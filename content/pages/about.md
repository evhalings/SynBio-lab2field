## Motivation

Working on water infrastructure projects in Central America with Engineers Without Borders, the most important lesson wasn't technical: sustainable development requires community independence. Good solutions are ones communities can operate, maintain, and adapt themselves. A solution that requires outside experts to return is not a sustainable solution.

### The gap

Synthetic biology is producing tools directly relevant to the problems many communities face — water quality, food safety, disease. But the lab-to-field pathway is often realized through opportunistic partnerships, and most scientific research is done within an academic bubble without local context.

## Research questions

1. What makes a synthetic biology technology field-deployable vs. lab-only?
2. What are the barriers to translating promising synbio research into real-world global development applications?
3. Where are the missing connections for technologies to reach communities that would benefit?

## Methods: multi-source research synthesis

- Case study analysis: three deployed or in-development synbio projects in global development contexts, analyzed using a consistent five-factor [deployment readiness framework]({{ base_url }}/researchers.html)
- Literature synthesis across synthetic biology, global health, humanitarian engineering, and development practice
- Stakeholder interviews across three groups (below)

### Stakeholder interviews

- **Synthetic biology researchers:** Julius Lucks (Northwestern), Keith Pardee (University of Toronto), and a researcher at Imperial College London
- **Global development practitioners:** practitioners at the Ifakara Health Institute (Tanzania) and Northwestern
- **Humanitarian engineering:** EWB Chicagoland chapter leadership and EWB-USA leadership

Interview themes: partnership structure, translation barriers, community ownership, and design for independence.
