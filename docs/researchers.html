For synthetic biology researchers

Why lab success doesn't guarantee field success

Synthetic biology is producing tools directly relevant to problems communities face — water quality, food safety, disease. But the lab-to-field pathway is often built through opportunistic partnerships, and most research happens inside an academic bubble without local context.

Across three case studies, the technologies that reached real communities shared a pattern: local partnership was built alongside the tool, not bolted on afterward.

Grounded in: experience on EWB water infrastructure projects in Central America — sustainable development requires community independence. A solution that requires outside experts to return isn't sustainable.

Three questions to ask throughout the process

1. Who is defining the problem?

You cannot tell a community what their problem is. The most successful tools here share one trait: the in-country partnership was built alongside the tool.

2. Independence or dependency?

Transferring tools from academic labs is faster. Building local capacity produces communities that can own the science.

3. How does local context change my design constraints?

Include social scientists in design. Explicitly consider how your design would change outside a well-equipped lab.

Five deployment readiness factors

  • Cold chain — independence from refrigeration
  • Partnership model — local co-design vs. co-execution
  • Regulatory path — environmental & political
  • Cost target — under $5–10 per use for most cases
  • Usability design — non-expert interpretable output

Where the connections aren't built yet

Agriculture

Cell-free biosensor tech is proven for small molecules. About 25% of Sub-Saharan African cereal crops are contaminated with aflatoxin annually, with no farm-level test.

Sanitation

Fecal biosensors exist, but EWB lacks community tools to verify latrine performance after deployment.
